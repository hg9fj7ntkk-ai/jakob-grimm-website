SCHALL UND HALL

In the beginning there is air. Before a note can sound, something has to flow: wind from the organ's bellows, breath from a human lung. Both find their way into a tube, and only there, in the narrowing, do they turn into sound. In the organ pipe, sound takes shape at a sharp metal edge or a vibrating reed; in the trombone, at the player's lips. At heart it is the same thing. The organ has known this for centuries. Among its stops is one that bears the name of the very instrument standing beside it here: Trombone.

There is a faint restlessness in an organ note, the breath of the wind, which you only notice when something steadier sounds alongside it. If the trombone follows that restlessness, it vanishes into the organ. If it resists, it stands out: a quiet disturbance in the fabric.

Alfred Schnittke caught the relationship between sound and space in two words. Schall, sound, is what a person sends out into a space. Hall, reverberation, is what the space makes of it: it gives it back, altered and delayed, and it no longer quite belongs to whoever made it. Here that space is St George's Minster in Dinkelsbühl, more than five hundred years old, a hall of stone that takes in every note and is in no hurry to let it go. The organ was built for this church. Its pipes were voiced here, for this particular stone and this particular air. It belongs to this place. The trombone belongs to no particular room. It has to find each one afresh.

Schnittke wrote his piece for just such a space, one that answers back. It begins in an ordered world. The organ sustains quiet chords, as though everything had already been said. Then the trombone enters and unsettles that order, and from there the music grows until, in the tumult, Schall itself becomes Hall. What remains at the end is a sound with barely any pitch left, an echo whose origin has slipped away.

Axel Ruoff asks a different question. For the orators of antiquity, rhetoric was at its most powerful in movere: the art of moving an audience, not merely instructing it but stirring something deep within it. Ruoff prefaces his piece with Cicero, who knew that people judge far more by love and hate, hope and fear, than by the truth. This music is not concerned with argument. It wants to set something in motion. Perhaps that is the simplest meaning of its title: before it moves a person, it moves air.

In the end it is a symbiosis, and the question is no longer which of the two is playing, but how long you can still hear the difference.

Jakob Grimm

Trombone

Jakob Grimm understands notated and improvised music as two paths to one sound that speaks directly. His bass trombone is an instrument of depth, rarely in the spotlight and for that reason full of undiscovered possibilities. Composers including Manuel Zwerger, Biagio Putignano and Mike Svoboda write for him, and broadcasters from ORF to BBC Radio 3 have featured his work. Concerts have taken him to Wigmore Hall in London, the Berlin Philharmonie and Grafenegg. With the quartet Tetra Brass he pursues a sound of his own between rediscovered repertoire and music written for the ensemble, and in music theatre, productions have taken him to the Bayerische Staatsoper and the Munich Biennale. He studied in Munich, Vienna, Amsterdam and Basel. He teaches trombone and chamber music at the Berufsfachschule für Musik in Dinkelsbühl and holds a teaching post for new music ensemble work at the University of Music and Performing Arts Munich.

Michael Spors

Organ

Michael Spors moves between the organ loft and the jazz stage, between scholarship and improvisation. Born in Hamburg in 1980, he studied church music, organ performance with Ludger Lohmann and music theory in Stuttgart, followed by musicology and philosophy in Tübingen, where he gained his doctorate with a study of Mozart's early symphonies. He completed his training with a master's degree in the theory of early music under Johannes Menke at the Schola Cantorum Basiliensis. In 2005 he founded the Improvision Piano Duo with Laurens Patzlaff, combining classically structured improvisation with jazz. His album J.azz – S.pors – Bach sets Bach's originals beside his own jazz arrangements. Since 2020 he has directed the Berufsfachschule für Musik in Dinkelsbühl, and he teaches music theory at the Stuttgart University of Music and Performing Arts, which appointed him honorary professor in 2021.
